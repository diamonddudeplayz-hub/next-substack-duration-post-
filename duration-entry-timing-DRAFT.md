On September 22 I asked value investors on Substack a question in a Note: how do you use technical analysis. Twenty-five answered, and almost all gave a version of the same thing. Fundamentals set the value, charts time the entry.

That is right, and it stops one step short. The step it misses has a name, and the name is duration. I agreed with the consensus when I read it, then went back through my own five positions to see how I actually time entries. How much the entry even mattered turned out to depend almost entirely on one thing, and on most value names it matters far less than the people timing them think.

## The short version

- The near-universal answer among value investors: fundamentals set the value, charts time the entry. It is right, and it stops one step short.
- Timing an entry only ever captures one part of a return. Your yield and your growth accrue with time no matter what day you buy. The only thing your entry price changes is the multiple you pay against the multiple you sell.
- How much that part is worth depends on how far out a company's value sits. Call that its duration. The further out, the more of the return is the multiple, and the more timing matters.
- On my own five names the spread is not subtle. Buy on a random day and hold a year and your outcome varied by a standard deviation of 18 points on Cheniere and 91 on NVIDIA.
- What would change my mind is below, and it is a number.

## A return has three parts

Break a holding-period return into its pieces. You earn the starting cash yield. You earn the growth in cash flow per share over the years you hold. And you earn, or lose, the change in the multiple between the price you paid and the price you sold.

The first two accrue with time. Buy a compounder on a Tuesday or three weeks later and the yield and the growth barely notice. The third is the only piece your entry price controls. So timing an entry is a bet on one thing: the change in the multiple.

That reframes the whole question. "When do I buy" is worth asking in proportion to how much of your expected return lives in the multiple, and how much that multiple swings while you wait.

## What duration is

Duration here means how much of a company's worth sits in cash flows that arrive years out rather than soon. A stock priced at a high multiple with little cash coming back today keeps most of its value in the back half of the next decade and beyond. I think of that as long duration, the same way a thirty-year bond is long duration. When the discount rate moves, its price moves more, and more of its value is the part the market is still only promising.

I expected the textbook arithmetic to carry the argument. On my own numbers, a plain ten-year discounted cash flow has a slow-growing value name losing about 13% of its value on a one-point rise in the discount rate and a fast-growing name losing about 20%, wider but not dramatic (the calculation is in the repo). Then I put my own names on the tape, and the gap is not modest at all. The reason the textbook understates it is below, in the data. The short version: the discount rate does not just discount the cash flows, it moves the multiple the market is willing to assign, and the multiple is most of a long-duration stock.

One line of regime, because it raises the stakes. The US ten-year yield was 5.29% at the start of September and 4.96% by October, with the thirty-year still above 5% and the term premium rising, the backdrop I laid out in the gold post. A higher and jumpier discount rate makes the multiple on long-duration equities swing harder, which makes a good entry worth more now than it was in the 2010s.

## My positions, in order of how much timing matters

**Cheniere (LNG).** The return is yield plus contracted growth and a small re-rating. Roughly 90% of production is contracted through the mid-2030s on a weighted-average contract life near 15 years, and $6.6B of fixed fees arrive over the next twelve months regardless of commodity prices. At 11 times contracted EBITDA that is a floor near $235. On top of the floor it hands back about 5.8% a year in cash, a 0.77% dividend plus a roughly 5% buyback yield, so part of the return lands in hand no matter what the multiple does. The downside is bounded by contract, not by when I bought. Timing barely matters here. The floor is the margin of safety, and the buy band is wide.

**Adobe (ADBE).** The return depends on a re-rating from 7.52 times EV/EBITDA back toward a 12.4 times peer group, and there is a real chance the cheapness is the diagnosis rather than the opportunity. Long duration, cash flows far out, no contractual floor under it. The stock touched $190 in June. The verdict, Q4 net new ARR and then the new CEO's first guidance in March, has not arrived. You cannot time an entry well when there is no dated catalyst and the thesis itself is still on trial.

**NVIDIA (NVDA).** A long-duration winner rather than a cheap re-rate. The return is mostly growth, and the risk is a multiple that falls if the shortage ends before the demand does, which is the Burry trade I wrote about. High duration and high rate sensitivity, so the entry price matters a lot, as the tape below shows more starkly than any name I own.

**Itron (ITRI).** The business is compounding on its own, with ARR up 21% and gross margin already past the 2027 target three years early. What makes it the most entry-sensitive name I own is that the single biggest piece of the upside is a multiple re-rate, from a 15.6 times hardware earnings multiple toward what a software business with these margins would command. The growth accrues regardless. The re-rate waits on the Investor Day, due around early 2027 once the Resiliency integration closes.

**Gold, held through Wheaton.** No cash flows, no price target by design, a floor that rises with central-bank buying. Timing on fundamentals is meaningless. You accumulate against a rising floor and watch the break conditions. It is the only name here with no price chart to run, which is the cleanest statement of the point.

## The Itron day that proves it

On July 28 Itron rose 26% in a single session on a margin beat and an EPS guide. I wrote at the time that the move repriced the EPS and did not reprice the business. In this framework that sentence has a precise meaning.

The 26% was the first two parts of the return. Earnings power got marked up. The multiple, the part a re-rating delivers, stayed at the bottom: 15.6 times, 0.75 times the S&P's forward multiple against a ten-year average of 1.29.

Here is what happened next, and it is the whole argument in one name. The stock has given the entire move back. Itron closed at $88.17 on October 22, below the $99.66 it settled at after the print and below my own cost. It now sits about 3% below its 200-week moving average, the exact level DiviStock Chronicles told me he accumulates quality names. The earnings pop faded because the multiple never re-rated, and the re-rate is still sitting out there, waiting on a catalyst with a date. Being early still costs me nothing. The piece I am waiting on has not moved.

## FOMO, catalysts, and sizing

This is where the chart people and the no-chart people stop disagreeing.

FOMO is a duration error before it is a feeling. It is paying the top multiple for the longest-dated cash flows right after the re-rate has happened for someone else, which is the worst moment to buy the one piece of the return your entry controls. The names where it hurts most are the long-duration ones, because that is where the multiple can fall furthest when the rate normalises.

A dated catalyst shortens duration. It pulls the re-rate forward in time, which raises what that piece is worth per year and cuts how long your capital sits idle. The Itron Investor Day, the Cheniere Q1 print, the expiry on a Burry put: each one puts a clock on the re-rate. Conviction sizing falls out of it. Size on the gap divided by the time to the catalyst. A wide gap with a short, dated clock earns size. A wide gap with no clock, Adobe or gold, earns patience instead.

## The tape, on my own five names

Everything above is a claim. Here is five years of closing prices testing it.

**How much did the entry date actually matter.** Take every trading day in the last five years, buy, hold one year, record the result. The spread of those outcomes is how much your entry date decided your fate.

| Name | worst 1-yr | median | best 1-yr | std dev |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cheniere (LNG) | −20% | +13% | +72% | 18 |
| Itron (ITRI) | −45% | +22% | +99% | 33 |
| Adobe (ADBE) | −58% | −21% | +105% | 38 |
| NVIDIA (NVDA) | −55% | +62% | +305% | 91 |

Entry date mattered five times as much on NVIDIA as on Cheniere, and Cheniere's worst year was a −20% while NVIDIA's was −55%. The short-duration name with the contracted floor is the one where timing was closest to noise.

**Drawdowns say the same thing, without a model.** Deepest five-year drawdown: Cheniere −26%, Itron −48%, NVIDIA −66%, Adobe −72%. The floored name fell least. The names whose return rides on a multiple fell two to three times as far. This is the robust version of the claim, and I lean on it rather than on any regression.

**Rate sensitivity points the same way.** Regress monthly returns on the monthly move in the ten-year yield and the ranking holds: Cheniere barely reacts, around 2% per one-point move, while the long-duration pair, Adobe and NVIDIA, move roughly 16 to 18%. Over forty months that regression is noisy, so I read it as a direction, not a decimal, and the drawdown ranking already said it without the math. What it is worth keeping is the reason the gap is so much wider than the textbook discounting implied: a rising rate does not just discount the cash flows, it compresses the multiple, and the multiple is most of a long-duration stock.

**Where the 200-week rule points right now.** DiviStock accumulates quality names at the 200-week moving average. Run it on the four names with price histories and it flags exactly one. Itron sits about 3% below its 200-week line today. Cheniere trades 43% above its line and NVIDIA 75% above, and neither has touched it in five years. Adobe sits 34% below a line still dragged up by its $600 era, which is a lagging average of a company that no longer exists, not a buy signal. The rule points at the one name where the re-rate has not happened, and says nothing useful on the names carried by growth or capped by a floor. One firing across five years is an observation, not a test. A real test wants fifteen years of weekly data and a cross-section of names, and that is a separate post.

Read against this, the Note answers stop arguing. Anibal Hernandez uses charts "to find entry and exit points," Rajveer Kapoor as "a position-sizing and timing layer, never the thesis," DiviStock at the 200-week line, Stockso Simple through volume. Those are all proxies for the sentiment swing in the multiple, the piece timing can capture, and they work on mean-reverting re-rate names. InvesTeacher, who ignores charts entirely because "in the long run, intrinsic value will always be reflected in the stock price," is right for a book of short-duration cash machines, where the multiple is a small and stable slice and there is nothing for a chart to add. The two camps are describing different durations.

## What would change my mind

If entry timing added the same return on the short-duration names as on the long-duration ones, the framework is wrong, and the dispersion table above is where it would show. If the share of return coming from the multiple were not higher for higher-multiple names, the engine underneath it is wrong. Price action on its own is never a reason to buy in any of this, only a read on where the multiple's part of the return might be.

## The close

The chart was never telling me when to buy. It was telling me how much of the return rode on the multiple. That was a duration question the whole time.
