On September 22 I posted a question in a Note: how do value investors on Substack use technical analysis. Twenty-five people answered. Almost all of them said a version of the same thing. Fundamentals set the value, technicals time the entry.

Anibal Hernandez, CFA: "I mainly use it to find entry and exit points, but my intrinsic value of the company is always determined by fundamentals." Rajveer Kapoor: "i use technicals as a position-sizing and timing layer, never the thesis." InvesTeacher, dissenting: "I don't use it... in the long run, intrinsic value will always be reflected in the stock price."

I agreed with the consensus when I read it. Then I went back through my own four positions to see how I actually time entries, and the consensus stopped one step short of the thing that decides it.

## The short version

- The near-universal answer among value investors: fundamentals set the value, charts time the entry. It is right, and it stops one step short.
- Timing an entry only ever captures one part of a return. Your yield and your growth accrue with time no matter what day you buy. The only thing your entry price changes is the multiple you pay against the multiple you sell.
- How much that part is worth depends on how far out a company's value sits. Call that its duration. The further out, the more of the return is the multiple, and the more timing matters.
- Across my four positions the honest answer to "when do I buy" runs from barely matters (Cheniere) to it is most of the trade (Itron).
- What would change my mind is below, and it is a number, not a feeling.

## A return has three parts

Break a holding-period return into its pieces. You earn the starting cash yield. You earn the growth in cash flow per share over the years you hold. And you earn, or lose, the change in the multiple between the price you paid and the price you sold.

The first two accrue with time. Buy a compounder on a Tuesday or three weeks later and the yield and the growth barely notice. The third is the only piece your entry price controls. So timing an entry is a bet on one thing: the change in the multiple.

That reframes the whole question. "When do I buy" is worth asking in proportion to how much of your expected return lives in the multiple, and how much that multiple swings while you wait.

## What duration is

Duration here means how much of a company's worth sits in cash flows that arrive years out rather than soon. A stock priced at a high multiple with little cash coming back today keeps most of its value in the back half of the next decade and beyond. I think of that as long duration, the same way a thirty-year bond is long duration. When the discount rate moves, its price moves more, and more of its value is the part the market is still only promising.

I ran the arithmetic expecting rate sensitivity to be the main divide. It was not. In a plain ten-year discounted cash flow, a slow-growing value name loses about 13% of its value on a one-point rise in the discount rate; a fast-growing name loses about 20%. Wider, but not the chasm I assumed.

The real divide is where the return comes from. A name trading at 15 times earnings earns you most of your return from yield and growth. A name trading at 35 times has to re-rate for the trade to work at all, which means the multiple, the one piece timing controls, is most of the return. Duration tells you which kind you are holding.

One line of regime, because it raises the stakes. In my September gold post the thirty-year Treasury was at 5.24% with the term premium rising. A higher and jumpier discount rate makes the multiple on long-duration equities swing harder, which makes a good entry worth more now than it was in the 2010s.

## My four positions, in order of how much timing matters

**Cheniere (LNG).** The return is yield plus contracted growth and a small re-rating. Roughly 90% of production is contracted through the mid-2030s on a weighted-average contract life near 15 years, and $6.6B of fixed fees arrive over the next twelve months regardless of commodity prices. At 11 times contracted EBITDA that is a floor near $235 against a price of $260 when I wrote it. The downside is bounded by contract, not by when I bought. Timing barely matters here. The floor is the margin of safety, and the buy band is wide.

**Adobe (ADBE).** The return depends on a re-rating from 7.52 times EV/EBITDA back toward a 12.4 times peer group, and there is a real chance the cheapness is the diagnosis rather than the opportunity. Long duration, cash flows far out, and no contractual floor under it. The stock touched $190 in June. The verdict, Q4 net new ARR and then the new CEO's first guidance in March, has not arrived. You cannot time an entry well when there is no dated catalyst and the thesis itself is still on trial.

**Itron (ITRI).** The return is almost entirely a multiple re-rate, from a 15.6 times hardware earnings multiple toward what a software business with its margins would command. The forcing function is the Investor Day, due around early 2027 once the Resiliency integration closes, when the model rebuild becomes hard to avoid. Here the entry is close to the whole trade.

**Gold, held through Wheaton.** No cash flows, no price target by design, a floor that rises with central-bank buying. Timing on fundamentals is meaningless. You accumulate against a rising floor and watch the break conditions.

It is one investor running one discipline across all four, and the variable that sorts the answers is duration.

## The Itron day that proves it

On July 28 Itron rose 26% in a single session on a margin beat and an EPS guide. I wrote at the time that the move repriced the EPS and did not reprice the business. In this framework that sentence has a precise meaning.

The 26% was the first two parts of the return. Earnings power got marked up. The multiple, the part a re-rating delivers, stayed at the bottom: 15.6 times, 0.75 times the S&P's forward multiple against a ten-year average of 1.29, roughly 7.8 times on the software layer when comparable software trades above 10. So after a 26% day, being early was still fine, because the piece I am waiting on had not moved. The mistake would have been chasing a name where the multiple had already re-rated. Ask which happened, the earnings or the multiple, and you have made most of the entry decision.

## FOMO, catalysts, and sizing

This is where the chart people and the no-chart people are both right.

FOMO is a duration error before it is a feeling. It is paying the top multiple for the longest-dated cash flows right after the re-rate has happened for someone else, which is the worst moment to buy the part of the return your entry controls. The names where it hurts most are the long-duration ones, because that is where the multiple can fall furthest when the discount rate normalises.

A dated catalyst shortens duration. It pulls the re-rate forward in time, which raises what that piece is worth per year and cuts how long your capital sits idle. The Itron Investor Day, the Cheniere Q1 print, the expiry date on a Burry put: each one puts a clock on the re-rate. Conviction sizing falls out of it. Size on the gap divided by the time to the catalyst. A wide gap with a short, dated clock earns size. A wide gap with no clock, Adobe or gold, earns patience instead.

## Back to the chart

Read the Note answers again with this in hand and they stop disagreeing.

DiviStock Chronicles buys quality names at the 200-week moving average: "That's a time where I accumulate heavily," plus the lower half of long-standing parallel channels. Stockso Simple reads volume to see "where the institutions are accumulating and distributing." Those are proxies for one thing, the sentiment swing in the multiple, which is exactly the piece of the return that timing can capture. They work on mean-reverting, re-rate-sensitive names, and they would do nothing for me on Cheniere, where the floor is contractual, or on gold, where the floor is rising rather than reverting.

InvesTeacher, who does not use charts at all, is right too, for a different book. If you own short-duration cash machines, the multiple is a small and stable part of your return, so there is almost nothing for a chart to add. The two camps are not arguing about technical analysis. They are describing different durations.

## The arithmetic

Illustrative multiples, author's calc. Shown so you can check it.

Decompose a three-year return into the part from yield and growth and the part from the multiple:

- An Itron-shaped trade, 15.6 to 25 times over three years on 7% cash-flow growth, returns about 25% a year, of which roughly 17 points, about two-thirds, is the multiple re-rate.
- A Cheniere-shaped trade, 10.4 to 11.5 times on 6% growth, returns about 10% a year, of which roughly 3 points, about a third, is the multiple.

The long-duration trade keeps two-thirds of its return in the one piece your entry controls. The short-duration trade keeps a third, and has a contractual floor under the rest. That, not rate sensitivity, is why timing matters more on one than the other.

And the cost of getting it wrong, against the cost of waiting too long:

- A 20% worse entry, amortised over the hold, costs about 7% a year over three years, 4% over five, 3% over seven.
- Missing a one-time 50% re-rate costs about 13% a year over three years, 8% over five, 6% over seven.

Missing the re-rate costs you close to twice what a bad entry does, and it is the long-duration names where the re-rate is the whole point. On the short-duration names the drag from a bad entry is real but small, and the floor caps the damage. The asymmetry is the argument.

## What would change my mind

If a clean backtest showed entry timing added the same return on short-duration names as on long-duration ones, the framework is wrong. If the share of return coming from the multiple were not higher for higher-multiple names, the engine underneath it is wrong. Price action on its own is never a reason to buy in any of this, only a read on where the multiple's part of the return might be.

## The close

The chart was never telling me when to buy. It was telling me how much of the return rode on the multiple. That was a duration question the whole time.

One question for readers who time entries: do you size the decision to how much of your return is the re-rate, or do you run the same entry discipline on every name regardless of what you are actually being paid for?
