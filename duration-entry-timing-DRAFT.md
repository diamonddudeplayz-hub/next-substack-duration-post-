On September 22 I asked value investors on Substack a question in a Note: how do you use technical analysis. Twenty-five answered, and almost all gave a version of the same thing. Fundamentals set the value, charts time the entry.

That is right, and it stops one step short. The step it misses has a name, and the name is duration. I agreed with the consensus when I read it, then went back through my own five positions to see how I actually time entries. How much the entry even mattered turned out to depend almost entirely on one thing, and on most value names it matters far less than the people timing them think.

## The short version

- The near-universal answer among value investors: fundamentals set the value, charts time the entry. It is right, and it stops one step short.
- Timing an entry only ever captures one part of a return. Your yield and your growth accrue with time no matter what day you buy. The only thing your entry price changes is the multiple you pay against the multiple you sell.
- How much that part is worth depends on how much of your return is cash paid to you no matter what, versus a future multiple the market may or may not grant. The more it leans on the multiple, the more timing matters.
- On my own five names the spread is not subtle. Buy on a random day and hold a year and your outcome varied by a standard deviation of 18 points on Cheniere and 91 on NVIDIA.
- What would change my mind is below, and it is a number.

## A return has three parts

Break a holding-period return into its pieces. You earn the starting cash yield. You earn the growth in cash flow per share over the years you hold. And you earn, or lose, the change in the multiple between the price you paid and the price you sold.

The first two accrue with time. Buy a compounder on a Tuesday or three weeks later and the yield and the growth barely notice. The third is the only piece your entry price controls. So timing an entry is a bet on one thing: the change in the multiple.

That reframes the whole question. "When do I buy" is worth asking in proportion to how much of your expected return lives in the multiple, and how much that multiple swings while you wait.

## What duration is

Duration means how much of a company's worth sits in cash flows that arrive years out rather than soon. A stock at a high multiple with little cash coming back today keeps most of its value in the back half of the next decade and beyond. On my own ten-year discounted cash flow, roughly three-quarters of a fast grower's value sits past year ten, in the terminal value the exit multiple stands in for. That is what makes it long duration, and it is why the price moves more when the discount rate moves: most of what you own is a number you have been promised, not yet paid.

One wrinkle, because Cheniere breaks the simple version. Its fee contracts run fifteen years, which by the textbook makes it long duration. It does not behave that way, because the cash is contracted and handed back along the way and a floor caps the repricing. So the axis that actually decides entry timing is not time-to-cash-flow. It is how much of your return is cash paid to you no matter what, against a future multiple the market may or may not grant. Committed cash on one end, a discretionary multiple on the other. The high-multiple names sit at the discretionary end, and that is where the entry decides the most.

I expected the textbook arithmetic to carry the argument. A plain ten-year discounted cash flow has a hypothetical slow grower losing about 13% of its value on a one-point rise in the discount rate and a fast grower about 20%, wider but not dramatic. Then I put my own names on the tape, and the gap is far larger, for a reason the textbook misses. A rising rate does not just discount the cash flows. It compresses the multiple the market will pay, and the multiple is most of a long-duration stock. More on that in the data.

One line of regime, because it raises the stakes. The US ten-year yield was 5.29% at the start of September and 4.96% by October, with the thirty-year still above 5% and the term premium rising, the backdrop from the gold post. A higher and jumpier discount rate makes the multiple on long-duration equities swing harder. That cuts both ways, and the second way is the opportunity. If the business is fine and only the rate moved, the swing hands you a better entry. Adobe fell to about $190 in June on nothing the business did. Buy it there and you are up a third today, with the actual question, the moat, still unresolved. The entry captured the swing, not the answer.

## My positions, in order of how much timing matters

**Cheniere (LNG).** The return is yield plus contracted growth and a small re-rating. Roughly 90% of production is contracted through the mid-2030s on a weighted-average contract life near 15 years, and $6.6B of fixed fees arrive over the next twelve months regardless of commodity prices (FY2025 10-K). At 11 times contracted EBITDA that is a floor near $235, which I built in the April post. On top of the floor it hands back about 5.8% a year in cash, a 0.77% dividend plus a roughly 5% buyback yield, so part of the return lands in your hand no matter what the multiple does. The downside is bounded by contract, not by when I bought. Timing barely matters here. The floor is the margin of safety and the buy band is wide.

**Adobe (ADBE).** Not a cash-flow risk. The revenue is recurring and the enterprise side is genuinely sticky, with switching costs around AEP and workflow lock-in. The duration here is in the narrative. The stock trades at 7.7 times EV/EBITDA against a software-peer median around 12 (CRM, ADSK, INTU, SAP, MSFT, ORCL, NOW), and the re-rate waits on one question resolving: whether the moat is bifurcating, the enterprise half defended while the individual creative half loses ground to open-source and AI-native tools. The new CEO frames that split on December 9, and Q4 net new ARR is the number that settles it. Until then the cheapness is a bet on a story, and you cannot time a story.

**NVIDIA (NVDA).** The cheapest of the long-duration names, about 14 times forward earnings, priced as if the AI build-out is about to slow. If it does not, the growth alone carries the return, and a re-rate on top is possible if the financing worries around its neocloud customers fade. The risk runs the other way: the multiple compresses if demand rolls over before the shortage does, the Burry trade I wrote about. Its worth rests on years of future demand, which is why the tape below shows the widest spread of outcomes by entry date of anything I own.

**Itron (ITRI).** The business is compounding on its own, ARR up 21% and gross margin already past the 2027 target three years early. What makes it the most entry-sensitive name I own is that the biggest piece of the upside is the market changing how it values the company at all: from an earnings multiple on a hardware business, about 12.9 times forward today, to an EV/ARR multiple on the software business it is becoming. That is the distance between $130 on EV/EBITDA and $326 on EV/ARR I laid out before. The growth accrues regardless. The re-rate waits on the Investor Day in early 2027, when the model rebuild gets hard to avoid.

**Gold.** The endpoint, and the cleanest version of the point. Physical gold has no cash flows and no price target by design, so there is nothing to time on fundamentals. You accumulate against a floor that rises with central-bank buying and watch the break conditions. I hold it mostly through Wheaton, with a smaller GLD position as a hedge for the gold-price risk that is independent of the miner. Wheaton is the exception that proves the rule. It does have cash flows, a streaming model and a P/OCF multiple, so it behaves like an equity with entry-timing risk, not like the metal. That one is a separate post.

## The Itron day that proves it

On July 28 Itron rose 26% in a single session on a margin beat and an EPS guide. I wrote at the time that the move repriced the EPS and did not reprice the business. In this framework that sentence has a precise meaning.

The 26% was the first two parts of the return. Earnings power got marked up. The multiple, the part a re-rating delivers, stayed where it was, an earnings multiple on a business the market still files under hardware.

Here is what happened next, and it is the whole argument in one name. The stock has given the entire move back. Itron closed at $88.17 on October 22, below the $99.66 it settled at after the print and below my own cost, which puts it at about 12.9 times forward earnings, cheaper than before the quarter that beat. It now sits about 3% below its 200-week moving average. The earnings pop faded because the multiple never re-rated, and the re-rate, the switch to valuing the software, is still sitting out there waiting on a catalyst with a date. Being early still costs me nothing. The piece I am waiting on has not moved.

## What actually makes a catalyst

A dated catalyst shortens duration. It pulls the re-rate forward in time, which raises what that piece is worth per year and cuts how long your capital sits idle. But a date on the calendar is not a catalyst on its own, and this is the part most timing talk skips.

If the market already expects a good print, a good print changes nothing. The expectation is in the price, and the stock needs a beat, and beating a bar the market set high is hard. The events that actually re-rate a stock are the ones that force the consensus to re-classify the business, not just to nudge an estimate. Itron reporting a strong quarter is priced. Itron presenting a segment-level framework that makes an analyst rebuild the model on EV/ARR instead of EBITDA is not, because the market is narrative-heavy and will not adopt your reading of a business until the evidence leaves it no choice. That is the second-level version of a catalyst. Not what happens, but what the market is made to admit it had wrong when it does.

Conviction sizing falls out of it. Size on the gap divided by the time to the catalyst. A wide gap with a short, dated clock earns size. A wide gap with no clock, Adobe or gold, earns patience instead.

## FOMO is a duration error

FOMO is a duration error before it is a feeling. It is paying the top multiple for the longest-dated cash flows right after the re-rate has happened for someone else, which is the worst moment to buy the one piece of the return your entry controls.

The names where it hurts most are the long-duration ones, because that is where the multiple falls furthest when the rate normalises or the story cracks. The October 8 selloff showed it cleanly. NVIDIA fell about 10% in the days after. CoreWeave and Nebius, the leveraged neoclouds whose whole value is a bet on future compute demand, fell about 31% and 30%, three times as far, on the same news. The longest-duration, most multiple-dependent names took the deepest cut, which is the same ranking the rest of this post keeps finding.

## The tape, on my own five names

Everything above is a claim. Here is five years of closing prices testing it.

**How much did the entry date actually matter.** Take every trading day in the last five years, buy, hold one year, record the result. The spread of those outcomes is how much your entry date decided your fate.

| Name | worst 1-yr | median | best 1-yr | std dev |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cheniere (LNG) | −20% | +13% | +72% | 18 |
| Itron (ITRI) | −45% | +22% | +99% | 33 |
| Adobe (ADBE) | −58% | −21% | +105% | 38 |
| NVIDIA (NVDA) | −55% | +62% | +305% | 91 |

Entry date mattered five times as much on NVIDIA as on Cheniere, and Cheniere's worst year was a −20% while NVIDIA's was −55%. The name carried by contracted cash and a floor is the one where timing was closest to noise.

**Drawdowns say the same thing, without a model.** Deepest five-year drawdown: Cheniere −26%, Itron −48%, NVIDIA −66%, Adobe −72%. The floored name fell least. The names whose return rides on a multiple fell two to three times as far. This is the robust version of the claim, and I lean on it rather than on any regression.

**Rate sensitivity points the same way.** Regress monthly returns on the monthly move in the ten-year yield and the ranking holds: Cheniere barely reacts, around 2% per one-point move, while the long-duration pair, Adobe and NVIDIA, move roughly 16 to 18%. Over forty months that regression is noisy, so I read it as a direction and not a decimal, and the drawdown ranking already said it without the math. What it is worth keeping is the reason the gap is so much wider than the textbook implied. A rising rate does not just discount the cash flows, it compresses the multiple, and the multiple is most of a long-duration stock.

**Where the 200-week line points right now.** There is a well-worn rule, usually hung on Charlie Munger though the quote is almost certainly apocryphal, that you buy quality names when they fall to their 200-week moving average. The 200-week part is the part people get wrong by reaching for the 200-day. A quality name touches its 200-day line constantly. It touches its 200-week line only in a real dislocation. Run the 200-week line on the four names with price histories and it flags exactly one. Itron sits about 3% below its 200-week line today. Cheniere trades 43% above its line and NVIDIA 75% above, and neither has been near it in five years. Adobe sits 34% below a 200-week line still dragged up by its $600 era, a lagging average of a company that no longer exists, not a buy signal. The rule points at the one name where the re-rate has not happened, and says nothing on the names carried by growth or capped by a floor. One firing across five years is an observation, not a test. A real test wants fifteen years and a cross-section of names, and that is a separate post.

Read against this, the survey answers stop arguing. The investors who use charts for entries are reaching for the same thing: a read on when the multiple, the sentiment piece, has swung too low and is likely to snap back. Those are the mean-reverting re-rate names, where the multiple oscillates around a level and buying it depressed catches the bounce, the Itrons and the Adobes. It does nothing on a short-duration cash machine, a tobacco name, a regulated utility, a contracted energy name like Cheniere, where the multiple is a small, stable slice and you are paid in cash regardless. The investor who ignores charts entirely is right for that book. The two camps are not disagreeing about technical analysis. They are describing different durations.

## What would change my mind

Two things would break this. If the names whose return is mostly cash paid to me rewarded good entry timing as much as the re-rate names do, the framework is wrong, and the dispersion table is where that would show. That is not really a short-versus-long cash-flow claim, since Cheniere's contracts run fifteen years. It is committed cash against a discretionary multiple. And if the higher-multiple names did not in fact get more of their return from the change in the multiple, the engine under all of it fails.

On the smaller point: a chart alone is never a reason to buy. It only tells you whether the multiple is depressed, which matters at all only once you have decided the business is worth owning.

## The close

The chart was never telling me when to buy. It was telling me how much of the return rode on the multiple. That was a duration question the whole time.
