On September 22 I asked value investors on Substack a question in a Note: how do you use technical analysis. Twenty-five answered, and almost all gave a version of the same thing. Fundamentals set the value, charts time the entry.

That is right, and it stops one step short. The step it misses has a name, and the name is duration. I agreed with the consensus when I read it, then went back through my own five positions to see how I actually time entries. How much the entry even mattered turned out to depend almost entirely on one thing, and on most value names it matters far less than the people timing them think.

## The short version

- The near-universal answer among value investors: fundamentals set the value, charts time the entry. It is right, and it stops one step short.
- Timing an entry only ever captures one part of a return. Your yield and your growth accrue with time no matter what day you buy. The only thing your entry price changes is the multiple you pay against the multiple you sell.
- How much that part is worth depends on how much of your return is cash paid to you no matter what, versus a future multiple the market may or may not grant. The more it leans on the multiple, the more timing matters.
- On my own five names the spread is not subtle. Buy on a random day and hold a year, and your outcome varied far more on one than the other: a standard deviation, the usual measure of how spread out the results are, of 18 points on Cheniere against 91 on NVIDIA.
- What would change my mind is below, and it is a number.

## A return has three parts

Break a holding-period return into its pieces. You earn the starting cash yield. You earn the growth in cash flow per share over the years you hold. And you earn, or lose, the change in the multiple between the price you paid and the price you sold.

The first two accrue with time. Buy a compounder on a Tuesday or three weeks later and the yield and the growth barely notice. The third is the only piece your entry price controls. So timing an entry is a bet on one thing: the change in the multiple.

That reframes the whole question. Timing your entry is worth the effort in proportion to two things: how much of your expected return comes from the multiple changing, and how much that multiple swings around while you hold. A large, volatile multiple component means a good or bad entry moves your return a lot. A small, stable one means it barely registers.

## What duration is

Duration means how far out a company's cash flows sit. The bond market has the cleanest version of it. A three-year zero and a thirty-year coupon bond are both bonds, but move the interest rate a point and the thirty-year swings far more, because more of what you are owed arrives further away. Stocks work the same way. A cheap, cash-generative name is the short bond: most of the value comes back to you soon, so a change in the discount rate barely moves it. A high-multiple grower is the long bond: most of what you are paying for arrives years out, past the end of any ten-year model, in the terminal value that the exit multiple stands in for. So its price moves more when the rate moves, and more of what you own is a number you have been promised, not yet paid.

One wrinkle, because Cheniere breaks the simple version. Its fee contracts run fifteen years, which by the textbook makes it long duration. It does not behave that way, because the cash is contracted and handed back along the way and a floor caps the repricing. So the axis that actually decides entry timing is not time-to-cash-flow. It is how much of your return is cash paid to you no matter what, against a future multiple the market may or may not grant. Committed cash on one end, a discretionary multiple on the other. The high-multiple names sit at the discretionary end, and that is where the entry decides the most.

I expected the textbook arithmetic to carry the argument, so I ran it. Take two companies worth the same today, both discounted at 9%. One grows its cash flow 3% a year, the slow grower. The other grows 15%, the fast one, with most of its value landing years out. Raise the discount rate a single point, to 10%. The slow grower loses about 13% of its value. The fast grower loses about 20%. Wider, the way duration says it should be, but not dramatic. That is the whole textbook effect, and everyone knows it, which is why a rate scare takes more out of long-duration names than short ones. It is also not the whole story. The arithmetic only moves the math. It does not move the mood, and when the rate rises the market drains the premium out of a long-duration multiple on top of the discounting, a bigger move than the math alone. Put my own names on the tape and the gap runs far wider than 13 against 20. More on that in the data.

One line of regime, because it raises the stakes. The US ten-year yield was 5.29% at the start of September and 4.96% by October, with the thirty-year still above 5% and the term premium rising, the backdrop from the gold post. A higher and jumpier discount rate makes the multiple on long-duration equities swing harder. That cuts both ways, and the second way is the opportunity. If the business is fine and only the rate moved, the swing hands you a better entry. Adobe fell to about $190 in June, on a management change and sour sentiment rather than the numbers, which looked fine. Buy it there and you are up roughly a third today, with the real question, the moat, no closer to an answer than it was. The gain came from the price swinging back, not from the business proving anything. The entry paid off before the thesis did.

## My positions, in order of how much timing matters

**Cheniere (LNG).** The rare name that offers yield, contracted growth and a re-rating all at once, with a floor under it. Roughly 90% of production is contracted through the mid-2030s on a weighted-average contract life near 15 years, and $6.6B of fixed fees arrive over the next twelve months regardless of commodity prices, part of $107.7B contracted in total (FY2025 10-K). Those fixed fees are close to pure margin. Put them on 11 times, a thesis-failure multiple, and that is a $72.6 billion enterprise value; strip $22.5 billion of net debt and $5.3 billion of CQP minority interest, which Cheniere consolidates but does not fully own, across 206.5 million shares, and the floor lands near $217 against a price of $287. On top of the floor it returns cash directly: a 0.8% dividend and a buyback that ran $1.1 billion in the first half, carrying the share count from 207 million toward a 175 million target, close to a 5% cash return that owes nothing to the multiple. And it is cheap, about 10.6 times next year's EBITDA (guided to $7.9 to $8.4 billion for 2026), because the market treats the war-driven margin spike as temporary. I think that underrates the compounding underneath: new trains and SPAs adding capacity, the 20/20 vision on DCF per share, the shrinking count. So this is committed cash and a low multiple at once, downside floored and upside from a re-rate the market has not paid for. Its five-year beta to the market is 0.01. Timing barely matters here, not for lack of upside but because a bad entry cannot cost you much.

**Adobe (ADBE).** Not a cash-flow risk. The revenue is recurring and the enterprise side is genuinely sticky, with switching costs around AEP and workflow lock-in. The duration here is in the narrative. The stock trades at 7.7 times EV/EBITDA, below every large-cap software peer in the set and cheaper even than Oracle at 9.2 times, against a peer median near 15.7 (Oracle, Intuit, Salesforce, Autodesk, Microsoft, SAP, ServiceNow). The re-rate waits on one question resolving: whether the moat is bifurcating, the enterprise half defended while the individual creative half loses ground to open-source and AI-native tools. The new CEO frames that split on December 9, and the Q4 numbers settle it, net new ARR and the enterprise ARR trend most of all, since how much of the freemium funnel converts is the open question. Until then the cheapness is a bet on a story, and you cannot time a story.

**NVIDIA (NVDA).** The cheapest of the long-duration names, about 14 times forward earnings and a low PEG alongside the rest of the chips, priced as if earnings are near a peak and the AI build-out is about to slow. If it does not, the growth alone carries the return, and a re-rate on top is possible if the financing worries around its neocloud customers fade. The risk runs the other way: the multiple compresses if demand rolls over before the shortage does, the Burry trade I wrote about. Its worth rests on years of future demand, which is why the tape below shows the widest spread of outcomes by entry date of anything I own.

**Itron (ITRI).** The business is compounding on its own, ARR up 21% and gross margin already past the 2027 target three years early. What makes it the most entry-sensitive name I own is that the biggest piece of the upside is the market changing how it values the company at all: from an earnings multiple on what it still files under hardware, a low-teens forward multiple today, toward what it would pay for the software business Itron is becoming. The growth accrues regardless. The re-rate waits on the Investor Day in early 2027, when the model rebuild gets hard to avoid.

**Gold.** Not the far end of this list. Off it entirely. Gold has no cash flows and no multiple, so there is nothing to value on fundamentals and nothing to time. The textbook would call a non-yielding asset the longest duration there is, and before 2022 that held: Western funds traded gold against real yields, and it fell when rates rose. That link broke. The marginal buyer is now central banks, who bought a record pace from 2022 through 2025 while yields climbed, and gold rallied into rising rates rather than falling with them. So I do not try to time it. I accumulate against a floor the central-bank buyer keeps lifting, and I watch the break conditions. I hold it mostly through Wheaton, which does have cash flows and a multiple and so does sit on the scale above, with a smaller GLD position to hedge the gold-price risk Wheaton carries on top.

## The Itron day that proves it

On July 28 Itron rose 26% in a single session on a margin beat and an EPS guide. I wrote at the time that the move repriced the EPS and did not reprice the business. In this framework that sentence has a precise meaning.

The 26% was the first two parts of the return. Earnings power got marked up. The multiple, the part a re-rating delivers, stayed where it was, an earnings multiple on a business the market still files under hardware.

Here is what happened next, and it is the whole argument in one name. The stock has given the entire move back. Itron closed at $88.17 on October 22, below the $99.66 it settled at after the print and below my own cost, and cheaper on forward earnings than it was before the quarter that beat. It now sits about 3% below its 200-week moving average. The earnings pop faded because the multiple never re-rated, and the re-rate, the switch to valuing the software, is still sitting out there waiting on a catalyst with a date. Being early still costs me nothing. The piece I am waiting on has not moved.

## What actually makes a catalyst

A dated catalyst shortens duration. It pulls the re-rate forward in time, which raises what that piece is worth per year and cuts how long your capital sits idle. But a date on the calendar is not a catalyst on its own, and this is the part most timing talk skips.

In a name everyone watches, you will not out-read the next earnings print. The bar is too well set, and if the market already expects a good print, a good print changes nothing. The expectation is in the price, the stock needs a beat, and beating a bar the market set high is hard. So the edge is never in the earnings. It is in a view the consensus does not hold yet, plus a reason it cannot hold it. The events that actually re-rate a stock do two things at once. They force the consensus to re-classify the business, and they remove the specific reason the consensus had not done it already. That second part is the one most timing talk skips, because if there were no reason the market was wrong, it would not be wrong. With Itron the reason is concrete: an analyst cannot build an EV/ARR model on a software segment the company only began breaking out three quarters ago, with no long-range framework to anchor it. The Investor Day is the catalyst precisely because it hands them that framework. So the test is not whether something good happens, which is usually priced, but whether it removes the barrier that kept your reading from becoming the consensus one. Itron reporting a strong quarter is priced. Itron giving analysts the segment framework to rebuild the model on EV/ARR is not, because the market is narrative-heavy and will not adopt your reading of a business until the evidence leaves it no choice. That is the second-level version of a catalyst. Not what happens, but what the market is made to admit it had wrong, once it can no longer explain the gap away.

Conviction sizing falls out of it. Size on the gap divided by the time to the catalyst. A wide gap with a short, dated clock earns size. A wide gap with no clock, Adobe or gold, earns patience instead.

## FOMO is a duration error

FOMO is a duration error before it is a feeling. It is paying the top multiple for the longest-dated cash flows right after the re-rate has happened for someone else, which is the worst moment to buy the one piece of the return your entry controls. Howard Marks put the other side of it plainly: "Everyone hopes a buyer will come along who's willing to overpay for what they have for sale. But certainly the hoped-for arrival of this sucker can't be counted on." Buying a long-duration name after its multiple has run is a bet that the sucker shows up.

The names where it hurts most are the long-duration ones, because that is where the multiple falls furthest when the rate normalises or the story cracks. The October 8 selloff showed it cleanly. NVIDIA fell about 10% in the days after. CoreWeave and Nebius, the leveraged neoclouds whose whole value is a bet on future compute demand, fell about 31% and 30%, three times as far, on the same news. The longest-duration, most multiple-dependent names took the deepest cut, which is the same ranking the rest of this post keeps finding.

## The tape, on my own five names

Everything above is a claim. Here is five years of closing prices testing it.

**How much did the entry date actually matter.** Take every trading day in the last five years, buy, hold one year, record the result. The spread of those outcomes is how much your entry date decided your fate.

| Name | worst 1-yr | median | best 1-yr | std dev |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cheniere (LNG) | −20% | +13% | +72% | 18 |
| Itron (ITRI) | −45% | +22% | +99% | 33 |
| Adobe (ADBE) | −58% | −21% | +105% | 38 |
| NVIDIA (NVDA) | −55% | +62% | +305% | 91 |

Entry date mattered five times as much on NVIDIA as on Cheniere, and Cheniere's worst year was a −20% while NVIDIA's was −55%. The name carried by contracted cash and a floor is the one where timing was closest to noise.

**Drawdowns say the same thing, without a model.** Deepest five-year drawdown: Cheniere −26%, Itron −48%, NVIDIA −66%, Adobe −72%. The floored name fell least. The names whose return rides on a multiple fell two to three times as far. This is the robust version of the claim, and I lean on it rather than on any regression.

**Rate sensitivity points the same way.** Regress each stock's monthly return on the monthly move in the ten-year yield over the last five years and the ranking holds. Cheniere barely reacts, and the long-duration names move about ten times as hard.

(Betas: Cheniere −1.6%, Itron −10.6%, NVIDIA −16.2%, Adobe −15.7% per one-point move in the ten-year, sixty monthly observations, October 2021 to October 2026. The R-squared runs from roughly zero on Cheniere, where rates explain almost none of the moves, to 0.16 on Adobe, so the relationship is directionally clear and numerically noisy. The drawdown ranking says the same thing without a regression, which is why I lead with it.)

What is worth keeping is why the gap is so much wider than the textbook implied. A rising rate does not just discount the cash flows. It compresses the multiple the market will pay, and the multiple is most of a long-duration stock.

**Where the 200-week line points right now.** There is a well-worn rule, usually hung on Charlie Munger though the quote is almost certainly apocryphal, that you buy quality names when they fall to their 200-week moving average. The 200-week part is the part people get wrong by reaching for the 200-day. A quality name touches its 200-day line constantly. It touches its 200-week line only in a real dislocation. Run the 200-week line on the four names with price histories and it flags exactly one. Itron sits about 3% below its 200-week line today. Cheniere trades 44% above its line and NVIDIA 77% above, and neither has been near it in five years. Adobe sits 34% below a 200-week line still dragged up by its $600 era, a lagging average of a company that no longer exists, not a buy signal. The rule points at the one name where the re-rate has not happened, and says nothing on the names carried by growth or capped by a floor. One firing across five years is an observation, not a test. A real test wants fifteen years and a cross-section of names, and that is a separate post.

Read against this, the survey answers stop arguing. The investors who use charts for entries are reaching for the same thing: a read on when the multiple, the sentiment piece, has swung too low and is likely to snap back. Those are the mean-reverting re-rate names, where the multiple oscillates around a level and buying it depressed catches the bounce, the Itrons and the Adobes. It does nothing on a short-duration cash machine, a tobacco name, a regulated utility, a contracted energy name like Cheniere, where the multiple is a small, stable slice and you are paid in cash regardless. The investor who ignores charts entirely is right for that book. The two camps are not disagreeing about technical analysis. They are describing different durations.

## What would change my mind

Two things would break this. If the names whose return is mostly cash paid to me rewarded good entry timing as much as the re-rate names do, the framework is wrong, and the dispersion table is where that would show. That is not really a short-versus-long cash-flow claim, since Cheniere's contracts run fifteen years. It is committed cash against a discretionary multiple. And if the higher-multiple names did not in fact get more of their return from the change in the multiple, the engine under all of it fails.

On the smaller point: a chart alone is never a reason to buy. It only tells you whether the multiple is depressed, which matters at all only once you have decided the business is worth owning.

## The close

The chart was never telling me when to buy. It was telling me how much of the return rode on the multiple. That was a duration question the whole time.

So the question is not whether to time your entries. It is which of your positions have earned the effort. Pull up your own book and sort it: the holdings where the return is cash you will be paid regardless, and the ones where it rides on the market changing its mind. Then ask how many you have been timing out of habit, when the return was never going to come from the multiple in the first place. Most people run it backwards.
